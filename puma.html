Room to Move

Question 3 — Puma connectivity

The puma question is the opposite of the bobcat one. There are too few puma records to model where the animals are, and the precise locations that do exist are sensitive enough that publishing them would do harm. So this section does not map where pumas were seen. It maps where the landscape would let one move — built from the shape of the land, not from sightings.

Built from resistance, not sightings

Every layer in this section rests on a resistance surface: a map of how costly each 1 km cell is for a puma to cross, high where the land is developed, steep, or busy with roads, low where it is protected open ground. The surface is assembled from land cover, terrain, human modification, and traffic — not from where anyone recorded a puma.

That has one consequence worth stating up front, because it is the mirror image of the bobcat result. The bobcat occupancy model had to correct for recording effort, because it was built from sightings. The puma corridors carry no effort term at all — there are no sightings in them to be biased. Where the puma corridors agree with a density surface, they agree for a structural reason, not because both were shaped by the same observers. This is the clean half of Question 5.

Why you see a band, not a line

The core result is a corridor between the two largest habitat blocks in the region: the Santa Cruz Mountains and the southern Diablo Range, linked across the south Bay through the Coyote Valley pinch where US-101 cuts the gap. The least-cost path between them runs 37.2 km and passes within 1.6 km of the verified Coyote Valley crossing.

But the maps below lead with a band, not that line, and the reason is a real limit, not a stylistic choice. A least-cost path is a single-cell-wide thread through a 1 km grid. It looks precise, and that precision is false: move the input weights a little and the thread shifts by a kilometre or more. The honest product is the swath — the zone of low-cost cells around the path — shown in two tiers:

  • a core band (the lowest-cost 2% of corridor cells, 409 km²) — high-confidence corridor,
  • a context band (the lowest-cost 5%, 1,021 km²) — the permeable flanks around it.

The swath says what the analysis can actually support: the corridor runs through here, in this zone, not the corridor is this exact line. The precise pinch at Coyote Valley is located separately, by road geometry in the crossing analysis below — not by the swath, whose 1 km grain is too coarse to resolve a sub-kilometre constriction.

Where the corridor meets the roads

A corridor is only as good as its worst crossing. Where the swath meets a major road, the road is a barrier whose severity scales with traffic, not just with presence — a quiet two-lane road and a freeway are not the same obstacle. So each crossing is ranked by traffic volume.

The top barrier is US-101 at Coyote Valley, at 142,000 vehicles a day. Getting that number right took a correction. An earlier version of the analysis, using a spatial traffic-data join, dropped US-101 here to a modelled floor of about 80,000 — a 43% underestimate — and let a lower-traffic arterial outrank it. The fix referenced the traffic data along the highway route by postmile, recovered the real 142k, and re-ran the corridor (Decision 34). US-101 is now correctly the top-ranked crossing.

One honest detail in that ranking: crossings are ranked within traffic-data confidence tiers, not on the raw number alone. A road whose volume is measured directly outranks one whose volume is filled in by name-matching, even when the filled-in number is close. This stops a lower-confidence estimate from masquerading as the top barrier — the exact failure the US-101 correction fixed.

The network, and a distinction that matters

Widening from the single corridor to the whole system of large habitat blocks — the 29 cores of at least 30 km² — gives a connectivity network: each core linked to its geometric neighbours, each link routed through the resistance surface. Two findings come out of it, and holding them apart is the point.

Coyote Valley is the efficient link, and the traffic barrier — but not the structural weak point. The network routes Santa Cruz to Diablo not as the forced direct line but as a cheap stepping-stone chain through the south-Bay cores, at roughly half the cost of the direct route. Those Coyote Valley cores are the efficient path; losing them forces the animal onto the costlier direct crossing. So Coyote Valley matters twice — as the real, protectable connection, and as the 142k traffic barrier astride it — but it is a strong link, not a weak one.

The structural weak links are the cross-bay spans. The costliest connections in the network are the long, high-resistance routes that would join the Peninsula / Santa Cruz cluster to the East-Bay / Diablo cluster across the central urbanized Bay — spans of 40 to 60 km with no stepping stones between them. The central Bay effectively splits the system into two subnetworks, joined efficiently only at the south end, through Coyote Valley. This matches what is already known: Santa Cruz Mountains pumas are famously isolated, connected to the Diablo Range mainly via the south Bay.

The traffic pinch and the structural weak point are therefore two different things on two different axes. Coyote Valley is a traffic-mitigation priority — a real corridor with a dangerous road across it. The cross-bay gaps are a fragmentation finding — evidence of what is already disconnected.

And the cross-bay links are not corridors to build. This distinction is load-bearing, so it is stated plainly: a 60 km least-cost path across the developed bayshore is at the edge of biological plausibility. The network method forces a route between geometric neighbours whether or not a real one exists, and the routing dutifully finds the least-bad line across an essentially impassable barrier. Those longest cross-bay links mean these clusters are effectively disconnected, not here is a corridor to protect. Read them as fragmentation evidence. The south-Bay Coyote Valley chain, by contrast, is a real connection worth protecting.

What this section is, and is not

These corridors are hypotheses about movement drawn from a landscape-cost model, not tracks from collared animals. No puma was followed to draw them; there is no telemetry here to confirm them. Shifting the model’s weights within a plausible range moves the corridor by up to about 1.5 km, so the routes are robust in location but not exact to the metre. They are the best structural estimate of where connectivity runs — a well-founded hypothesis, and the right basis for asking where to look and where to protect, but a hypothesis.

Every puma layer here is shown as generalised geometry — bands and routes, never points. That is deliberate and required: precise puma locations are a poaching and disturbance risk, so the project never publishes them. Nothing in this section is fine enough to reverse-engineer a den, a kill site, or a camera. The corridors are about where an animal could move, at the scale of the landscape, not about where any individual animal is.
