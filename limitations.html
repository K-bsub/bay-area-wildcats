What this project cannot tell you

Limitations, stated plainly

Good analysis is clear about its own limits. This page collects the ceilings that apply to the maps on this site, in one place, so they are not buried in captions or lost between pages. None of them is a reason to distrust the work — each is a reason to read a specific result at its true strength, no more.

The limitations fall into two kinds: what the corridor can and cannot claim, and what the descriptive maps (density, hot spots) can and cannot claim. A short note on the data inputs follows.

The corridor is a hypothesis, not a tracked path

The puma corridor is the single result most likely to be over-read, so it gets the plainest statement.

  • No animal was tracked. The corridor is built entirely from the landscape — land cover, human modification, slope, and traffic. This project has no GPS-collar or telemetry data of its own. So the corridor is a modelled hypothesis about where movement is cheapest, not a record of where pumas actually went. Strong validation would need collar data showing animals using the route; this project cannot provide that, and does not claim it.
  • The route is weighted by author-set priors. The resistance surface blends its inputs on weights chosen by the analyst (45% human modification, 40% land cover, 15% slope), with directions and the traffic transform taken from published Santa Cruz Mountains puma work. The weights are sourced and reasonable, but they are priors, not fitted values — this project has no local movement data to fit them against.

How much the route can move: about 1.5 km of play

The corridor’s location is stable; its exact centre-line is not. When the gHM/land-cover weight was perturbed by ±10% (a pre-registered sensitivity test), the broad corridor barely changed — the worst-case overlap of the wide context band stayed very high (IoU 0.941). But the single least-cost line shifted, and under gHM-heavy weighting the path shortened from 37.2 km to 35.6 km, a route play of about 1.5 km. This is exactly why the corridor is published as a two-tier swath, not a line: the band is honest about the play, the line is not.

NoteRead the band, not the line

A 1 km analysis grain is also coarser than the features it crosses. At the Coyote Valley pinch, the least-cost line passes about 1.6 km from the exact pinch cell — not an error, just the grain. The swath locates the corridor regionally; the road-crossing step, not the centre-line, locates the pinch.

The core-connectivity network has a cross-bay artifact

A second connectivity product — the network graph linking large habitat cores — carries a specific, named artifact that must not be over-read.

  • The network is built with a Gabriel graph, which connects cores that are geometric neighbours. Across the central Bay, that forces edges over the water, which is an impassable barrier for a puma. So the longest cross-bay links in the network are not candidate corridors — they are evidence that those clusters are disconnected, which is the opposite of a route to protect.
  • What survives this caveat: the ranking of links by cost is valid within the graph, the two-subnetwork finding (the central Bay splits a Peninsula / Santa Cruz Mountains subnetwork from an East Bay / Diablo one, joined efficiently only at the south) is real and matches known Santa Cruz Mountains puma isolation, and the contrast between cheap south-Bay links and costly cross-bay links holds. What does not survive: reading any individual cross-bay link as a protectable corridor. The site’s network map shows the cost-ranked edges and flags the cross-bay links with this caveat; it does not draw a labelled route across the bay.
NoteA correction on the record

An earlier version of this network stated a specific node count and a specific core-to-core chain. Both were wrong — the stated cutoff disagreed with the code as run, and the chain was never actually traced. The error was found, documented, and corrected, with the original text preserved for audit. The ranking and the two-subnetwork finding were unaffected and still stand. The correction is recorded in the methodology log (Decisions 37–38).

The descriptive maps are shaped by where people looked

The density (KDE) and hot-spot (Gi*) maps describe records, and records are made by people, unevenly. This is the effort problem, and it is the subject of its own page — but it belongs in any honest list of limits.

  • Every descriptive surface is effort-shaped. A warm density zone or a red hot spot can mean “many animals” or “many observers.” The two tracks handle this differently — the bobcat occupancy model removes the effort signal, so it diverges from the raw density; the puma corridor never contains an effort signal, so its convergence with the raw density is informative rather than circular — but no descriptive map escapes it.
  • The puma hot-spot map is about half effort. Of the puma record hot-spots, only a minority survive the effort cross-check as trusted; the rest coincide with survey effort and are flagged suspect on the map. A red unit is not proof of pumas until that check clears it.
  • The convergence is corroboration, not proof. Where the corridor and the record density agree, part of that agreement is structural — both rest on the same land-cover and human-modification gradient — so it is read as weak-to-moderate corroboration, not validation.

Data-input limits worth stating

These are smaller, but each shapes a specific result and each is carried openly rather than smoothed over.

  • Chaparral is under-mapped — but it does not move the corridor. The land-cover source (ESA WorldCover) badly under-maps California chaparral, prime cat habitat. This sounds serious for a habitat model. A pre-registered sensitivity test bounded it: rebuilding the resistance surface with chaparral treated as good habitat changed the corridor essentially not at all (overlap IoU 1.000 / 0.998). So the under-mapping is immaterial to connectivity — stated because it is real, not because it changes the result.
  • Traffic counts are biased upward off the highways. Measured traffic covers state highways; local and arterial roads are filled in from the nearest measured segment or a class-based floor (about 19,851 segments spatially filled, and roughly 45% on a modelled floor). Because count stations cluster on busy roads, that fill runs high. The effect is compressed by the log-inverse traffic transform and bounded by a sensitivity check, but it is a real property of the data, tracked with a source flag on every segment rather than hidden.
  • The analysis grain is regional. The puma surfaces are built at a 1 km grain, which is coarse for sub-kilometre features like a single road pinch. The surfaces are regional context; the crossing-point analysis, not the surface, resolves the fine detail.

Two whole questions left open

  • Road mortality is incomplete. The wildlife–vehicle collision question is currently answered as a risk-overlap proxy, because record-level roadkill data is still pending. The collision-risk page says so directly and is marked incomplete.
  • No population trend is possible. There is no repeated regional census of either species. A one-time statewide puma estimate exists as context; no statewide bobcat estimate exists at all. Nothing on this site should be read as a population trend.

Every limitation above is recorded in full, with its sensitivity test and its figures, in docs/methodology.md. The point of gathering them here is the project’s first principle: state the ceiling, do not bury it.
